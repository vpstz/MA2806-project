Tobacco use in the UK

In the UK, smoking is no longer as prevalent as it once was. It has been banned from taking place in pubs, restaurants, offices, and public buildings, and now the government is banning the sale of cigarettes to anyone born after 2008. That made me wonder: is smoking already disappearing, or is it simply changing who it affects?

To explore this, I used UK smoking prevalence data by demographic groups, alongside health and mortality data linked to smoking. Together, these datasets will show a story of decline

A Habit in decline

Despite decades of health warnings, smoking remains a significant public health issue in the UK, even though it is much less common than it used to be.

The overall trend shows that adults smoking across each year is decreasing

Smoking has declined for both men and women since 2000, but men have stayed consistently more likely to smoke than women.

Age tells the strongest story in this dataset. At the start of the chart, younger adults, especially those aged 16-24 and 25-34 had some of the highest smoking rates. Over time, these younger groups have fallen below most of the older group. With the 16-24 group falling from consistently being the top second smokers, to now being second to least likely to smoke.

This is important because the UK's newer tobacco policies are focused on preventing future generations from smoking. The data suggests that younger people are already moving away from smoking before the new law fully takes place. Which makes sense why the government is trying to make a smoke-ree generation now; as it follows a trend that has already started.

This breakdown shows that smoking has declined across most groups but, the pattern is not completely even. An interesting thing to note, is that only the unknown category has changed positions in whom most likely smokes, whereas the others seem to mostly stay at a relatively similar position while decreasing.

The white and mixed demographics are shown to consistently be the top smokers, whereas groups like Asian, Chinese and black are consistently at the bottom. This suggests that smoking is shaped by social and cultural factors, not just individual choice.

The effects of smoking

Smoking is often discussed as a physical health issue, but its effects spread further than just the body. It can affect many other things like appearance, finances, relationships, mental health and overall quality of life.

The damage is not always immediate, which can make the risks seem distant and not as worrying as they should be, especially to young people.

Physical affects

Smoking has a range of negative effects on your body, ranging from shortness of breath/asthma and reduced fitness, to long term illnesses that can become life-threatening. The mortality data below shows that smoking is connected to many different parts of the body, not just the lungs.

Mental health

Most smokers take a cigarette to try and help focus or calm themselves, but it can actually have the opposite effect. It only gives you a short burst of nicotine providing the feeling of relaxation, before quickly exiting your system and leaving you feeling slightly worse than when you started. This can lead to a vicious cycle where you feel like you need to keep taking it otherwise your anxiety and depression could get worse.

Appearance and daily life

Some effects are less visible in statistics, but still matter in everyday life: stained teeth, aging skin, smell, reduced stamina and the cost of buying tobacco. These efefcts help explain why quitting is not only a medical issue, but also a social and personal one.

People with more sensitive noses may end up not wanting to spend time with you because of the smell.

Deaths

The smoking usage data shows a decline, but the deaths linked to smoking show why tobacco is still a major public health issue. The chart shows that smoking-related deaths are not caused by one single illness, but a large range of illnesses.

The largest categories suggest that the most series effects of smoking are often long term diseases. This is important because smoking can look like an individual habit in the present, while the consequences come to catch you later on in life.

The difference between the number of total smoking related deaths of men and women is that there appears to be more men who are killed by smoking than women. However, this is inline with the different numbers of how much either sex smokes. As our chart earlier showed that men were more likely to smoke than women. which explains this difference.
